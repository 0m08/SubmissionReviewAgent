# Style: Direct Trade Explainer

**Source course:** Plumbing Fixture & Components
**One-line description:** Plain, matter-of-fact prose that walks a technician component-by-component through a fixture, naming trade terms plainly and closing most slides with a forward-pointing "here's what's next" sentence.

> **Paragraph shape is a house convention, not a style property.** The
> single-paragraph norm measured below reflects the source course. Where the
> team's shared editorial standards prescribe a paragraph shape — short
> paragraphs of one or two sentences separated by blank lines, for scannability
> on mobile — that prescription governs, in this style and every other.

## Orthography & punctuation

- **American spelling** throughout (no British forms found: "color" not "colour" style choices are absent from the text, but American conventions like fraction-hyphenation and "toward"-style constructions are used consistently). No spelling divergence was found worth flagging beyond standard American usage.
- **Quotation marks are straight, not curly**, and used sparingly to introduce trade jargon/nicknames, always with terminal punctuation placed inside the closing quote (American style):
  - `In the trade, a bathroom sink is a "lavatory" or "lav."` (Topic 1, "The Lavatory System")
  - `Faucets can be identified by their mounting configuration, or "spread," which is the distance between...` (Topic 1, "Faucet Spreads and Parts")
- **Apostrophes are mostly straight (`'`)** — `home's`, `you'll`, `toilet's` — but a handful of curly `'` slipped through inconsistently (e.g. `home's supply and drainage systems` in "The Lavatory System" uses a curly apostrophe while the very same slide also uses straight ones elsewhere). Treat straight `'` as the target; don't worry about hunting down the rare curly one.
- **No em dashes or en dashes appear anywhere in the 45 slides analyzed.** Hyphens are used only for compound modifiers and fractions (`4-inch`, `one-piece`, `top-mount`, `1-1/2-inch`, `90-degree`). Do not introduce em dashes when mimicking this style — use a period or comma instead.
- **No serial-comma violations observed**; lists of three or more items consistently use the Oxford/serial comma: `supply lines, drainage, and appliances`; `angle, straight, multi-turn, and quarter-turn stops`.
- **Numbers:** measurements, dimensions, and technical specs are always digits, even under ten: `4-inch hole spacing`, `8 inches`, `3/8-inch connection`, `12-inch distance`, `1/2-inch supply lines`, `90-degree angle`, `2-inch drain pipe`, `360° bowl wash`. Small non-technical quantities are sometimes spelled out even alongside a digit in the same sentence: `A fixture's design determines if a repair takes 20 minutes or two hours.` (Topic 6). Abbreviations for units/ratings are always spelled out on first use with the abbreviation in parentheses: `GPF (Gallons Per Flush)`, `GPM (Gallons Per Minute)`, `bath waste and overflow (BW&O)`, `PVD (Physical Vapor Deposition)`.
- **Semicolons are rare** (4 across 45 slides) — used only to join two closely related independent clauses, e.g. `Touchless faucets are a common upgrade you'll see; they use sensors to turn the water on with a wave of a hand.` (Topic 5). Don't overuse them when mimicking.
- **Title Case in headings, sentence case in body.** Ampersands (`&`) occasionally substitute for "and" in titles only, never in body text: `Valves & Its Mechanisms`, `Water Efficiency & Flow Performance`, `Fill Performance & Homeowner Experience`.

## Sentence & paragraph shape

Measured across all 45 slides (9 Transition, 30 Content, 6 Summary) in Topics 1–6:

- **Overall average: 16.6 words per sentence**, 75.0 words per slide.
- **By slide type:**
  - Transition: avg 52.1 words/slide, 3.1 sentences/slide, 16.8 words/sentence, 317.2 chars/slide.
  - Content: avg 87.2 words/slide, 5.3 sentences/slide, 16.5 words/sentence, 539.1 chars/slide (the longest and most information-dense type).
  - Summary: avg 48.3 words/slide, 2.7 sentences/slide, 18.1 words/sentence, 374.8 chars/slide.
- **Paragraph count per slide is essentially always 1** (avg 1.0–1.03 across all types) — bodies are single flowing paragraphs. Only one slide in the whole sample breaks into two paragraphs (Topic 6, "Shutoffs and Connectors", split with a single line break after the second sentence). Do not default to multi-paragraph bodies; one paragraph is the norm.
- Sentence length varies moderately within a slide — short declarative sentences (5–10 words) are mixed with longer compound/complex ones (25–35 words) that stack multiple clauses with commas, e.g. the 44-word sentence in "Visible Drain Components": `The P-trap is the most important part of the drainage system.` immediately followed by shorter sentences, then a longer one — the rhythm alternates short-punchy / long-explanatory rather than staying uniform.

## Person & voice

- **Mixed person, technician-centered.** Second person ("you"/"you'll") appears in 15 of 45 slides (33%) — used to address the learner directly when giving practical/field advice, not for basic definitions. Count: 20 instances of "you", 1 of "your" across the whole corpus.
  - `you'll encounter several different sink styles in residential homes` (Topic 1)
  - `You'll commonly find supply stops beneath bathroom sinks, kitchen sinks, or near toilets.` (Topic 3)
  - `When servicing these, remember that they involve electronic components that standard faucets don't have.` (Topic 5)
- **Third person ("the technician") is used for more formal/summary framing**, especially in Summary slides: `helps technicians identify connections, diagnose common issues, and perform installation and maintenance more effectively` (Topic 1 summary); `a technician can't simply wipe away` (Topic 5).
- **Mostly active voice**, with passive used deliberately for process/mechanism description where the actor is irrelevant: `The mounting shanks are threaded tubes extending under the sink` / `the toilet is secured to the drain by the closet flange`.
- **Tone is plain, instructional, and unembellished** — no jokes, no exclamation points, no rhetorical flourishes. It reads like a field manual explained conversationally, not a marketing script and not a dry spec sheet. Confident, declarative statements dominate ("X is Y", "X does Y") over hedged language.

## Opening / hook pattern

Transition slides (9 total) open in one of two ways, split roughly evenly:

1. **A direct statement of the concept/stakes**, then narrowing toward the topic:
   - `A bathroom sink is a bridge between the home's pressurized water supply and its gravity-fed waste system.` (Topic 1)
   - `Proper drainage is about more than just getting rid of water.` (Topic 4)
   - `Imagine a leaking faucet with no dedicated water supply shutoff at the fixture.` (Topic 3 — the one scenario/imagine-style opener in the sample)
2. **A statement followed by an explicit "let's" forward-pointer** — this pattern appears **three separate times** and is NOT avoided in the final corrected slides:
   - `Bathrooms have the most plumbing connections in a house. They require a precise mix of supply, waste, and vent lines. Let's understand how.` (Topic 2, "Bathroom Layouts")
   - `The materials and finishes you choose for a fixture determine how long it will last and how much maintenance it will need over its lifetime. Let's look at what's happening beneath the surface of the faucets and basins you'll see in the field.` (Topic 5, "Choosing the Right Materials")
   - `Being service-ready means looking at a fixture and knowing exactly what it takes to fix or replace it before you ever pick up a tool. Let's look at the practical details that make a job go smoothly.` (Topic 6, "Preparing for Service")

**Correction to the earlier analysis note:** an earlier document (`agents/slide_chunk_editor/analysis/04-plumbing-fixture-components-slides.md`) claimed this reviewer bans meta-narration openers like "Let's look at...". **The final, human-corrected Topic tabs do NOT confirm this ban** — "Let's understand how" and "Let's look at..." both survive into the final approved text, in 3 of the 9 Transition slides (33%). Do not treat "Let's look at" as forbidden for this course; it is a legitimate, recurring hook pattern in the approved content. (It's possible the ban applied to an earlier draft/rule that was later relaxed, or to a stricter phrasing like "Let's dive into" that doesn't appear here — but as written, "Let's look at" and "Let's understand" are present and final.)

## Transition pattern

Nearly every Content and Transition slide ends with a sentence that bridges to the next slide's topic, rather than just summarizing itself. This is one of the strongest, most consistent patterns in the corpus:

- `...you'll encounter several different sink styles in residential homes.` → next slide covers sink styles. (Topic 1)
- `While sink styles vary, the faucet mounted on them can also differ in design and installation.` → next slide covers faucets. (Topic 1)
- `Regardless of the faucet style, water follows the same basic path through the fixture.` → next slide covers the supply/drainage path. (Topic 1)
- `After water leaves the faucet, it enters the kitchen drainage system.` → next slide covers kitchen drainage components. (Topic 1)
- `Regardless of the toilet style, every toilet connects to the home's plumbing system in a similar way.` → next slide covers toilet connection points. (Topic 2)
- `But just as important is understanding how efficiently the fixture uses water...` → next slide covers water efficiency. (Topic 5)

When mimicking: end most Content/Transition slides with a one-sentence pivot that names (explicitly or implicitly) what the next slide will cover, using connective phrasing like "Regardless of...", "While X varies...", "After X, it...", "But just as important...".

## Title conventions

- **Title Case** (major words capitalized) — consistent across all 45 titles.
- **Average 3.5 words per title**, range 2–5 words. Short and functional, never a full sentence, never a question.
- **Plain/functional, not metaphorical or clickbait-y** — titles name the subject of the slide directly rather than teasing it.
- **Review/Summary slides are always titled "[Topic Name] Review" or "[Topic Name] Summary"** — a fixed pattern: `Sinks and Faucets Review`, `Common Bathroom Fixture Review`, `Supply Stops and Connectors Review`, `Drainage and Flushing Review`, `Materials and Performance Review`, `Service Readiness Summary`.
- Real titles quoted verbatim:
  - `The Lavatory System`
  - `Common Sink Styles`
  - `Faucet Spreads and Parts`
  - `Toilet Connection Points`
  - `Valves & Its Mechanisms`
  - `Moving Waste and Stopping Smells`
  - `Fill Performance & Homeowner Experience`
  - `Troubleshooting in the Field`
  - `Service-Friendly Fixture Design`

## Formatting

- **No bold, no italics, no markdown formatting of any kind** in the body text.
- **No bullet points, no numbered lists anywhere in the final content** — even when a slide enumerates several sink styles, faucet types, or valve types, it does so in flowing prose sentences (`The Drop-in sink has the rim that sits on top of the counter. The Under-mount sink is attached to the bottom of the counter...`), not a bulleted list.
- **Single paragraph per slide is the default.** Only one exception in the whole sample uses a line break to split into two paragraphs (Topic 6, "Shutoffs and Connectors").
- No inline links, no footnotes, no special characters beyond straight quotes, hyphens, `&`, `/` (for fractions), and `°` (for degrees).

## Representative examples (verbatim, do not paraphrase)

**Example 1 — Content**
Title: `Faucet Spreads and Parts`
Type: Content
Body:
> Faucets can be identified by their mounting configuration, or "spread," which is the distance between the centers of the mounting holes or handles. A centerset faucet is the most common style, with the spout and handles mounted on a single base designed for 4-inch hole spacing. A widespread faucet has separate handles and spout installed in individual mounting holes, typically spaced 8 inches or more apart. Faucets can also be identified by their internal control mechanism. Single-handle faucets usually use a cartridge or ceramic disc to control and mix hot and cold water. Double-handle faucets use separate control assemblies for hot and cold water, which may be valve stems with washers, cartridges, or ceramic disc valves, depending on the design. Regardless of the faucet style, water follows the same basic path through the fixture.

**Example 2 — Transition**
Title: `Preparing for Service`
Type: Transition
Body:
> Being service-ready means looking at a fixture and knowing exactly what it takes to fix or replace it before you ever pick up a tool. Let's look at the practical details that make a job go smoothly.

**Example 3 — Summary**
Title: `Drainage and Flushing Review`
Type: Summary
Body:
> Drains, traps, and toilet fill and flush components all play essential roles in fixture operation and serviceability. Understanding how water flows through these components helps technicians isolate fixtures, perform repairs safely, and quickly identify the source of leaks, clogs, or flushing issues.
