# Style: Scenario-Driven Technician Voice

**Source course:** Plumbing Specialty Tools
**One-line description:** Short, plain-spoken paragraphs that drop a working plumber into a specific job scenario, then walk them tool-by-tool through what to grab and why, addressed directly as "you."

## Orthography & punctuation

- **American spelling is the baseline.** "PSI" (all caps, not "psi"), "leak-resistant," "gauge," "flushometer." Only one lapse into British spelling found in the entire corpus: "food colouring" / "coloured water" in the *Toilet Dye Tests* slide (Topic 5) — treat this as a one-off inconsistency to fix, not a house convention, when mimicking the style (i.e., write "coloring"/"colored" going forward).
- **Apostrophes are inconsistent/unnormalized** — straight `'` and curly `’` both occur in roughly equal numbers (12 vs. 12) across the corpus, e.g. `"You're installing..."` (straight) vs. `"Let’s recap"` (curly). Either is acceptable when mimicking; don't treat this as a signal to normalize.
- **No em dashes or en dashes appear anywhere in the corpus.** Zero occurrences of `—` or `–`. Compound modifiers use a plain hyphen instead: `"leak-resistant"`, `"hard-to-reach"`, `"tight-fixture"`, `"slow-draining"`, `"drop-head attachment"`.
- **No serial-comma test case appears** (lists of 3+ items with "and" are rare), but short lists like `"dry, secure, and leak-free"` use the Oxford comma.
- **Numbers:** small/round numbers are spelled out in running prose only rarely; measurement/spec numbers use digits: `"45-degree flare"`, `"PSI"`. No large numbers appear in the sample. Units are lowercase words ("pounds per square inch, or PSI") introduced once, then abbreviated.
- **No bold, no markdown emphasis, no bullet characters anywhere in the body text.** Every slide is plain prose paragraphs — see Formatting section.
- Tool names on first mention are followed by a plain-language alias in a comma clause: `"a basin wrench, also called a sink or faucet wrench,"`; `"a toilet auger, also called a closet auger,"`; `"soldering, sometimes called sweating,"`. This is a very consistent, repeatable pattern.

## Sentence & paragraph shape

Measured across all 47 slides (Topics 1–5, final/corrected tabs only; Transition/Content/Summary types; sentence-split on `.!?` boundaries, paragraph-split on newline):

| Slide type | n slides | avg paragraphs/slide | avg chars/slide | avg sentences/paragraph | avg words/sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transition | 9 | 2.33 | 364 | 1.71 | 15.1 |
| Content | 34 | 1.88 | 303 | 1.75 | 15.8 |
| Summary | 4 | 1.75 | 393 | 2.14 | 15.9 |

Arithmetic: for each slide type, body text was split into paragraphs on `\n`; paragraph count and character count were averaged over the n slides of that type. Each paragraph was then split into sentences; sentence count per paragraph and word count per sentence were averaged over the pooled sentences (64 sentences / 1,772 words for Content, 21 sentences / 545 words for Transition, 7 sentences / 239 words for Summary).

Takeaways: sentences run **~15–16 words** regardless of slide type — short and single-clause or two-clause. Paragraphs are **short, 1–2 sentences**, and a slide typically has **2–3 short paragraphs** (Transitions run slightly longer than Content). Summary slides pack slightly more per paragraph (2.1 sentences) because they're compressing a full topic's tools into one recap block.

## Person & voice

**Confirmed baseline: second-person direct address to the technician is the correct register for this course** (per the cross-course finding — this is a hands-on procedural/tools course, not a systems/concept course). "you" appears 42 times across the corpus; "your" only once. This is not a defect to remove — it is the house voice.

- Direct address puts the learner on the job: `"You're installing a new plumbing fixture, and it's time to connect the water line."`
- Imperative/step voice is used freely inside Content slides once the scenario is set: `"Start by making a clean, square cut..."`, `"Attach the pressure gauge to the outdoor faucet, then turn on the water..."`, `"Position the light so it illuminates the area..."`, `"Wipe the repaired connection dry and watch for new moisture."`
- First-person plural ("we"/"our"/"let's") is used sparingly and only as a **presenter's guiding voice**, almost always in "Let's" openers: `"Let's begin with PEX..."`, `"Let's start at the sink."`, `"Let's recap."` — 10 occurrences of "Let's/Let's" total, concentrated at slide/section openings, not mid-paragraph.
- Voice is overwhelmingly **active**, not passive: "A basin wrench... has a long handle and a pivoting jaw that help it reach..." rather than "the mounting nuts can be reached by...". Passive constructions do occur but are functional/descriptive ("is designed to," "is located") rather than the dominant mode.
- Tone is plain and matter-of-fact, not chatty or exclamatory — no jokes, no exclamation points found in the corpus, no marketing language.

## Opening / hook pattern

Transition slides consistently open with a **you-are-there scenario**, not a definition or an abstract statement. Verbatim openings:

- `"You're installing a new plumbing fixture, and it's time to connect the water line."` (Topic 1, Making Reliable Connections)
- `"You're servicing a plumbing fixture when you discover that the fastener or fitting is hidden in a tight space."` (Topic 2, The Challenge of Tight Fixture Work)
- `"Let's say you're scheduled for a service call. At one job, you find a tub draining slowly because of a clog. At another, you need to recover a small object lodged inside the drain piping."` (Topic 3, Common Drain Problems)

A minority of Transitions instead open with a direct factual/problem statement rather than a "you" scenario — e.g., `"Routine faucet and valve repairs often involve stuck components, recessed parts, or fittings in tight spaces where standard tools may not work well."` (Topic 4) — but even these pivot to "you" by the second sentence (`"...you'll match these specialty tools to common faucet and valve service tasks."`).

## Transition pattern

Slides bridge from the prior idea with an explicit connective clause or callback rather than jumping cold into new content:

- `"Once the tubing is prepared, you're ready to make the connection."` → next slide begins the connection method.
- `"Unlike a flaring tool, a swaging tool expands..."` — contrast-word bridge ("Unlike X, Y...") is a recurring device, also seen in `"Unlike PEX, some copper connections require..."` and `"Unlike clearing a clog, the goal is to retrieve the object..."`.
- `"But what if the clog is inside the toilet trap?"` — a rhetorical-question bridge that pivots from the prior tool to the next one.
- `"So far, we have looked at standalone tools used to clear clogs or retrieve objects. In some cases, instead of using a different tool, plumbers can change the attachment..."` — explicit recap-then-pivot.
- Summary slides always open with an explicit recap marker: `"Let's recap."` / `"Let's recap what you learned."`

## Title conventions

Titles average **3.4 words** (range 1–7 words across 47 slides). Style is **Title Case** and plain/functional — naming the tool or the task, not a metaphor or a question. No title in the corpus is phrased as a question.

Representative titles:
- `"PEX Specialty Tools"` (2 words)
- `"The Basin Wrench"` (3 words)
- `"Crimp Connection Tools"` (3 words)
- `"Faucet Multi-Function Wrench"` (3 words)
- `"The Challenge of Tight Fixture Work"` (6 words — longer titles appear on Transition slides that frame a scenario)
- `"Understanding PSI"` (2 words)
- `"Verifying Soldered Copper"` (3 words)
- `"Summary"` (1 word — Summary slides are sometimes titled with a topic-specific phrase like "Toilet and Drain Tool Summary," and sometimes just the bare word "Summary")

Pattern: Content-slide titles are almost always **Tool Name + generic noun** ("X Wrench," "X Tools," "Verifying X"). Transition-slide titles are more scenario/framing phrases ("The Challenge of...", "The Need for...", "Seeing the Problem", "Confirming the Repair").

## Formatting

- **No bold, no italics, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no markdown of any kind** appear anywhere in the 47 slides analyzed. All content is flowing prose broken into short paragraphs by blank lines (`\n\n` between paragraphs within a chunk).
- Multi-step procedures are written as **sequential prose sentences within a paragraph**, not a numbered list — e.g., `"Before flaring, make a clean, square cut, remove any burrs, and slide the flare nut onto the tubing."` The sequence is implied by sentence order and connective words ("Once...", "After...", "Before...", "Then..."), never by literal numbering.
- Paragraph breaks are used to separate: (1) the scenario/setup, (2) the tool introduction, (3) how it's used/why it matters — a loose but consistent three-beat structure on Content slides.
- Slide bodies are short: 1–3 paragraphs, rarely more.

## Representative examples (verbatim, do not paraphrase)

**Example 1 — Content slide, typical tool-introduction structure**
Title: `The Basin Wrench`
Type: `Content`
Body:
```
Let's start at the sink. When installing, removing, or servicing a faucet, you may need to tighten or loosen the mounting nuts that secure it to the sink.

These nuts are typically located beneath the sink, where access is limited.

A basin wrench, also called a sink or faucet wrench, has a long handle and a pivoting jaw that help it reach and grip the mounting nuts in tight spaces.

This makes it easier to tighten or loosen the mounting nuts, even when there is very little room to work.
```

**Example 2 — Transition slide, scenario-hook opening**
Title: `Common Drain Problems`
Type: `Transition`
Body:
```
Let's say you're scheduled for a service call. At one job, you find a tub draining slowly because of a clog. At another, you need to recover a small object lodged inside the drain piping. Although both situations involve a drain, each requires a different approach and specialty tool. Let's look at how the right tool can help you handle each situation.
```

**Example 3 — Summary slide, recap-and-close structure**
Title: `Summary`
Type: `Summary`
Body:
```
Working on faucets, toilet seats, and flush connections often means working in tight or hard-to-reach spaces.

Specialty tools are designed to reach, grip, or turn specific fasteners and fittings where access is limited.

Choosing the right specialty tool for the job makes service easier while helping prevent damage to the fixture or its finish.
```
