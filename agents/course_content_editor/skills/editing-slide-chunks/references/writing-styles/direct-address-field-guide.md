# Style: Direct-Address Field Guide

**Source course:** Water Line Material Identification
**One-line description:** Short, single-topic-per-slide prose that speaks to the technician as "you" in the moment of doing the job, moving from a real-world trigger ("when you're servicing...") to the plain fact the technician needs to know.

## Orthography & punctuation

- **American spelling and units throughout** — "PSI," "colored," no British forms observed. No spelling divergence found worth flagging.
- **Apostrophes are straight (`'`), not curly**, confirmed by direct character inspection: `you'll`, `it's`, `that's`, `they're`, `pipe's`. Do not introduce curly quotes when mimicking.
- **No em dashes and no en dashes anywhere in the 27 slides analyzed** (explicit character count: 0 of each). Hyphens (17 total) are used only for compound modifiers/labels: `Pressure-Reducing Valves`, `potable-water approval`, `fixture stop`. Use a period, comma, or new sentence instead of a dash when mimicking this style.
- **No bullet points and no bold/markdown formatting anywhere** — every slide body is plain flowing paragraphs (0 `*` characters found in the whole corpus). Do not add bullets or bold when mimicking.
- **Numbers: digits for measurements and ratings, words for small ordinary counts.** Measurements and technical values are always digits: `100 PSI`, `73°F`, `120°F`, `NSF-61`. The one small-count case in the corpus is spelled out: `two simple checks`. Follow "digits for specs, words for small ordinary quantities."
- **"PSI" is written fully capitalized** (`100 PSI at 73°F`), not lowercase "psi." Degree symbol is written tight against the number with no space: `73°F`, `120°F` — spelled out only once as backup phrasing in the same sentence ("100 pounds per square inch") to restate the abbreviation for clarity, not as the default form.
- **Colons and semicolons are almost never used** (1 colon, 0 semicolons across the whole corpus) — the one colon appears in a title (`Transition Fittings: Dielectric Unions`), not in body prose. Compound ideas are built with new sentences or commas, not semicolons.
- **Serial comma used consistently** in lists of three or more: `ball, gate, fixture shutoff, check, and pressure-reducing valves`; `sleeves and plastic grommets` (two-item lists use plain "and").
- **Contractions are the default, not the exception**: `you'll`, `it's`, `that's`, `they're`, `don't`, `shouldn't`, `aren't`, `won't` appear throughout body text — this is conversational prose, not formal writing.

## Sentence & paragraph shape

Measured across all 27 slides analyzed (Topic 1: 9, Topic 2: 7, Topic 3: 11 — includes one untitled continuation row treated as Content) — 4 Transition, 20 Content, 2 Summary, 1 unlabeled continuation slide:

- **Overall average: ~15.6 words per sentence** across the corpus (Content 14.9, Transition 14.1, Summary 20.9 — summaries run noticeably longer per sentence, stacking clauses to recap multiple ideas).
- **By slide type (arithmetic: summed words-per-sentence / total sentences, and chars per body / count of bodies, per type):**
  - Transition (n=4): avg 14.1 words/sentence, ~1.1 sentences/paragraph, ~3.2 paragraphs/slide, ~319 chars/slide.
  - Content (n=20): avg 14.9 words/sentence, ~1.5 sentences/paragraph, ~2.9 paragraphs/slide, ~395 chars/slide.
  - Summary (n=2): avg 20.9 words/sentence, ~1.2 sentences/paragraph, ~3.0 paragraphs/slide, ~480 chars/slide.
- **Paragraphs per slide is consistently 3, not 1** — this style breaks each slide into several short paragraphs (usually 1–2 sentences each) separated by blank lines, rather than one long flowing paragraph. Example (Topic 1, "Check Valves"):
  ```
  Some valves work automatically instead of being operated by hand. One of the most common is the check valve.

  A check valve allows water to flow in only one direction. If water tries to flow backward, the valve closes automatically to prevent reverse flow and keep water moving in the intended direction.
  You'll commonly find check valves in sump pump, and well systems.
  ```
- Sentences are short and declarative, rarely exceeding ~20 words; the longest sentences occur in Summary slides where multiple ideas get chained with commas (`Also look for places where different pipe materials meet.`).

## Person & voice

- **Second person, direct and constant.** "You" appears 34 times and "you'll" 16 times across 27 slides (roughly 1–2 instances of "you"/"you'll" per slide) — this is a defining trait, not an occasional device.
  - `When you're servicing a residential plumbing system, you'll notice valves and fittings at different points along the piping.` (Topic 1, "Intro")
  - `You'll recognize it by its lever handle.` (Topic 1, "Ball Valves")
  - `Take a closer look at a plastic pipe, and you'll see a line of text running along its side.` (Topic 2, "Locating the Printline")
- **Imperative/instructional asides addressed to the reader**: `Always check the fitting markings to confirm they're approved for the exact materials being joined.`; `Before installing one, check the label or markings to confirm its intended use and temperature rating.`
- **Mostly active voice.** Passive appears only for mechanism description where the actor doesn't matter: `each one has an important job`, `water flow is stopped`-type constructions are rare; more typical is active: `A check valve allows water to flow in only one direction.`
- **Tone is plain, practical, field-manual conversational** — no jokes, no exclamation points, no marketing flourish. Confident short declaratives dominate ("X is Y," "X does Y," "You'll find X"). Reads like a mentor talking a trainee through the job in real time.

## Opening / hook pattern

Transition slides (including topic "Intro" slides, which function as the opening hook) open by placing the learner inside a task, then narrowing to what's coming next. Verbatim openings:

- `When you're servicing a residential plumbing system, you'll notice valves and fittings at different points along the piping.` (Topic 1, "Intro")
- `Imagine you're about to install or replace a plastic water pipe.` (Topic 2, "Intro")
- `Installing a plumbing system involves more than simply connecting pipes.` (Topic 3, "Intro")
- `Most residential water heaters are typically set around 120°F, although some systems may store or deliver water at higher temperatures.` (Topic 3, "The Temperature Rule")

Nearly every Intro/Transition slide ends with an explicit forward pointer sentence, often starting with "Let's": `Let's start by exploring the different types of valves and the role each one plays.`; `Let's see how to quickly identify the correct pipe before getting started.`; `Let's explore which pipes, fittings, and connectors are intended for hot water, cold water, or both, and how to protect plumbing components from damage.`

## Transition pattern

Slides bridge from the prior idea by naming what was just covered, then pivoting with "But," "Sometimes," "Not all," or a contrast word before introducing the new concept — a mini recap-then-pivot structure rather than a hard cut:

- `Ball and gate valves control water flow to an entire line. Sometimes, though, you only need to shut off water to a single fixture, like a sink or toilet.` (Topic 1, bridges from valve types into fixture shutoff valves)
- `Not all automatic valves control the direction of water flow. Some help control water pressure instead.` (Topic 1, bridges from check valves into pressure-reducing valves)
- `The same check applies to pipe fittings.` (Topic 3, bridges from pipe suitability into fitting suitability — explicit callback with "the same check")
- `Choosing the right pipe, fitting, and connector is only part of the job. Once installed, they also need protection from the environment and from damage within the building.` (Topic 3, bridges from compatibility topic into the protection topic)

## Title conventions

Titles are short (1–5 words), functional/plain rather than metaphorical or clickbait, and almost always Title Case. Real titles from the corpus:

- `Ball Valves`
- `Check Valves`
- `Pressure-Reducing Valves`
- `Transition Fittings: Dielectric Unions` (colon-separated subtitle format used once)
- `Locating the Printline`
- `Understanding Pipe Size`
- `Potable Water Approval`
- `Water supply pipes - PEX and copper` (one lowercase/sentence-case outlier with a hyphen separator, an inconsistency worth noting but not the dominant pattern)
- `Nail Plates and Noise Control`
- `Topic Summary` (used verbatim as the fixed title for Summary-type slides; plain "Summary" also appears once)
- `Intro` (used verbatim as the fixed title for the first slide of a topic)

No titles are phrased as questions. Titles name the component/concept being discussed, not a benefit or a hook.

## Formatting

- No bold, no italics, no bullet lists — 100% plain prose paragraphs.
- Bodies are broken into short paragraphs (2–4 typically) separated by blank lines (`\n\n`); occasional single `\n` line breaks appear within what reads as one paragraph, and some slides have a trailing blank line at the end of the body text.
- "Intro" is the fixed title for a topic's opening Transition slide; "Topic Summary" (or "Summary") is the fixed title for the closing Summary slide — reuse these exact labels rather than inventing new ones.

## Representative examples (verbatim, do not paraphrase)

**Example 1 — Content slide (Topic 1, "Ball Valves")**

> One of the most common valves you'll come across is the ball valve. If you need to quickly shut off water to a main line or branch line, you'll typically use a ball valve.
>
> You'll recognize it by its lever handle. If the handle is parallel to the pipe, the valve is open, and water flows. If it's perpendicular to the pipe, the valve is closed, and the flow stops.

**Example 2 — Transition slide (Topic 2, "Intro")**

> Imagine you're about to install or replace a plastic water pipe.
>
> The pipe may look right, but appearance alone isn't enough.
>
> Before making the connection, you need to confirm that it's suitable for the application.
>
> Let's see how to quickly identify the correct pipe before getting started.

**Example 3 — Summary slide (Topic 3, "Topic Summary")**

> Before making any plumbing connection, take a moment for these two simple checks:
> First, make sure the pipe, fitting, and connector are all suitable for the water they'll carry.
>
> Then, protect the plumbing system with the right materials to reduce heat loss, freezing, abrasion, and other sources of damage.
>
> Following these steps helps keep the plumbing system safe, reliable, and performing efficiently for years to come.
