# Style: Guided Walkthrough

**Source course:** Plumbing System Fundamentals
**One-line description:** Plain, second-person narration that follows a single concrete scenario (a drop of water, a flushed toilet) step by step through a system, using short declarative sentences and almost no formatting.

## Orthography & punctuation

- **Spelling is American by default**, with one stray British form: "Vent pipes admit air into the drainage system to **equalise** pressure" (Topic 3, "Why Vent Pipes Are Needed"). Treat this as a one-off slip, not a rule — everywhere else spelling is American (e.g., "traveling," "modeled," standard American mechanical terms). Do not deliberately mix spellings; default to American and don't repeat the "equalise" anomaly.
- **Straight quotes and apostrophes only** — 18 straight `'`/`"` marks found, zero curly/smart quotes. Contractions use the straight apostrophe: `Let's`, `it's`, `home's`, `side's`.
- **No em dashes and no en dashes appear anywhere in the corpus.** Hyphens are used only inside compound words/terms (`main shut-off valve`, `hot-water piping`, `cross-connection`, `to-go coffee cup`), never as a sentence-interrupting dash. Do not introduce em dashes when mimicking this style — use a period, comma, or parenthetical instead.
- **Serial (Oxford) comma is used consistently** in lists of three or more: "fittings, valves, and fixtures"; "kitchens, bathrooms, and laundry spaces"; "sinks, dishwasher, toilets, shower, and clothes washer"; "safe, sanitary, and reliable."
- **Units/abbreviations are spelled out on first use, then abbreviated in caps**: "Water pressure, measured in PSI (pounds per square inch), is the force inside the pipes..." — `PSI` is capitalized, not `psi`.
- **Numbers are always spelled out in prose, never digits** — no bare digit numerals (e.g., "0-9") appear anywhere in the 21 slides analyzed; quantities are described qualitatively ("a small amount of water," "multiple fixtures") rather than with numerals.
- No bold, italics, or other inline emphasis markup is used in body text (zero `**` markers found across the corpus).

## Sentence & paragraph shape

Measured across all 21 slides (5 Transition, 15 Content, 1 Summary; Topic 1 = 9 rows, Topic 2 = 4 rows, Topic 3 = 8 rows), splitting on blank-line breaks for paragraphs and on `.`/`!`/`?` for sentences:

| Slide Type | n | Avg words | Avg chars | Avg paragraphs | Avg sentences | Avg words/sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transition | 5 | 87.2 | 525.8 | 4.2 | 5.2 | 16.8 |
| Content | 15 | 73.8 | 458.5 | 3.8 | 4.7 | 15.8 |
| Summary | 1 | 88.0 | 556.0 | 4.0 | 6.0 | 14.7 |

- Transition slides run slightly longer than Content slides (more setup needed to frame the scenario) and use noticeably more paragraph breaks (avg 4.2 vs 3.8).
- Sentences average **~16 words**, consistently in the 15-17 word range regardless of slide type — short-to-medium, single-clause-heavy sentences are the norm, not long compound sentences.
- Paragraphs are typically **1-3 sentences long**; a slide body is built from several very short paragraphs (often single-sentence) rather than one dense block. Example (from "Trap Seals Create a Barrier"): each of the 4 sentences is its own paragraph.
- Slide bodies routinely open with a **short, isolated one-sentence paragraph** that sets the scene before the explanatory paragraphs follow, e.g. "Imagine water draining from a kitchen sink." stands alone as its own paragraph before the mechanism is explained.

## Person & voice

- **Direct second person ("you") appears in Transition and Summary/Recap slides** to address the learner: "When you pull the plug in a sink or flush a toilet..."; "Knowing where the main shut-off valve is located allows **you** to quickly stop the water supply..."; "**You** explored how the P-trap and trap seal prevent sewer gases..."; "**You** also learned what backflow and cross-connections are..." Content slides mid-topic are more often third-person/impersonal ("the water flows," "the pressure regulator reduces").
- **First-person plural ("we"/"let's") is used to narrate the walkthrough itself**, functioning like a tour guide: "**Let's** follow the journey of a single drop of water..."; "Now that we know what moves the water, **let's** follow its journey through a home."; "**Let's** review the journey."; "**Let's** recap."; "To understand how plumbing systems help preserve trap seals, **we** need to look at another important part..."
- Voice is **overwhelmingly active**: "The curb stop allows the water supply... to be turned on or off," "Vent pipes admit air into the drainage system," "The trap seal forms a barrier." Passive constructions appear only occasionally and briefly ("is mounted," "is installed," "is typically located").
- Tone is **plain and instructional, not chatty** — no jokes, no exclamation points, no rhetorical flourishes beyond the occasional direct question. Register sits between a technical explainer and a friendly field-training voice (comparisons to everyday objects like a "to-go coffee cup" or "garden hose left submerged in a bucket" ground abstract mechanisms in familiar experience).

## Opening / hook pattern

Transition slides consistently open with one of two moves — a **concrete everyday scenario** or a **direct question** — never with a dry topic statement:

- Scenario-first: "A homeowner turns on a kitchen faucet to fill a glass of water." (Water's Journey)
- Scenario-first: "Imagine walking into a home and smelling sewer gas coming from a sink drain." (Why Doesn't Sewer Gas Come Back Through the Sink?)
- Time/condition-first, near-scenario: "When you pull the plug in a sink or flush a toilet, the wastewater seems to disappear almost instantly." (What Keeps Wastewater Moving?)

Titles of Transition slides are frequently phrased as questions ("What Keeps Wastewater Moving?", "Why Doesn't Sewer Gas Come Back Through the Sink?"), and the body's opening scenario sets up that question before answering it.

## Transition pattern

Slides bridge from the prior slide's idea with an explicit **forward-reference phrase** rather than silently jumping to new content — usually placed as the slide's first or second sentence:

- "**Now that we know what moves the water**, let's follow its journey through a home." (From Source to Home, following The Push Side)
- "**After passing through the main shut-off valve**, water often flows through a pressure regulator." (Pressure Regulator, chaining directly off the prior slide's last component)
- "**The fixture drain is just one part of the drainage system.** It connects to a larger branch drain..." (From Branch Drain to Building Drain — restates the previous slide's endpoint before extending it)
- "**To understand how plumbing systems help preserve trap seals**, we need to look at another important part of the drainage system: vent piping." (Why Plumbing Systems Need Vents, explicitly linking back to the trap-seal concept just covered)

Recap/Summary slides open by naming the act of recapping itself: "Let's review the journey." / "Let's recap." / "Let's recap." — always signaled, never implicit.

## Title conventions

- **Title Case** throughout (each major word capitalized): "Main Shut-Off Valve," "Hot and Cold Water Supply," "Trunk and Branch Piping," "Protecting the Water Supply," "How an Air Gap Prevents Backflow."
- Average title length is **~4 words** (range 1-9 across 21 titles; the two 1-word outliers are both "Recap").
- Titles are mostly **plain/functional noun phrases naming the component or concept** ("Pressure Regulator," "The Push Side," "Trunk and Branch Piping") rather than punny or metaphorical — the one metaphorical exception is "The Push Side" (framing the supply system via a push/pull metaphor introduced in the body).
- A minority of titles (all Transition slides) are phrased as **questions**: "What Keeps Wastewater Moving?", "Why Doesn't Sewer Gas Come Back Through the Sink?", "What Is Backflow?"
- Summary slides are titled simply "Recap" or a "From X to Y" journey-recap phrase ("From Source to Fixture").

## Formatting

- Body text is **plain prose paragraphs only** — no bullets, no numbered lists, no bold/italic emphasis, no headers within the body.
- Paragraphs are separated by a **blank line** (double newline), and short (often single-sentence) paragraphs are the norm — this creates a stepped, one-idea-per-paragraph rhythm rather than dense blocks.
- Some rows have stray leading/trailing blank lines or double blank lines within the body (e.g., "Main Shut-Off Valve" opens with two blank lines before the first sentence) — this looks like formatting noise from editing rather than an intentional pattern; don't reproduce extra blank lines deliberately.
- No inline markdown links, images, or special characters are used in the body text.

## Representative examples (verbatim, do not paraphrase)

**1. Title:** Water's Journey
**Type:** Transition
**Body:**
```
A homeowner turns on a kitchen faucet to fill a glass of water.

At the same time, a toilet is flushed, and a dishwasher is running. Even with multiple fixtures using water at once, water continues flowing everywhere it's needed.

But, how does water reach every fixture in the building the moment it's needed?

To answer that question, let's follow the journey of a single drop of water as it travels from its source, through the home's plumbing system, and finally out of a fixture.
```

**2. Title:** Pressure Regulator
**Type:** Content
**Body:**
```
After passing through the main shut-off valve, water often flows through a pressure regulator.

The pressure regulator reduces incoming water pressure to a level that is safe for the home's plumbing system. Excessive pressure can place unnecessary stress on pipes, fittings, valves, and fixtures.

Once the pressure has been adjusted, water is ready to be distributed throughout the building.
```

**3. Title:** Recap
**Type:** Content (Summary-function slide, Topic 3)
**Body:**
```
Let's recap. 

You explored how the P-trap and trap seal prevent sewer gases from entering the building, and how vent piping maintains proper airflow to protect those trap seals and keep wastewater flowing smoothly. 

You also learned what backflow and cross-connections are, and how an air gap helps prevent contaminated water from flowing back into the potable water system. 

Together, these components help keep plumbing systems safe, sanitary, and reliable.
```
